### 3.1 Step 1: Detrending

The algorithm includes a dynamic detrending filter required for data preprocessing. Detrending ensures that the data being examined is not affected by trends or one-time events. Extracting linear trends from time series data is a necessary precondition for successful cycle analysis. In the economic cycle literature, the Hodrick and Prescott (1980) filter (HP filter) has become the standard method for removing long-term movements, such as trends, from data. Hodrick and Prescott proposed the HP filter to decompose macroeconomic time series data into cycle and trend components. The HP filter assumes that movements in time series are composed of a smooth and slowly changing trend component. By removing this trend component from the data series, the filter provides the pure underlying cyclical behavior.

Visually, this detrending technique is like drawing a smooth linear trend line through plotted chart data and extracting this "freehand" trend line from the full dataset. The resulting component relies solely on cyclical behavior without the underlying trend. Now, we can proceed and begin additional cycle analysis in the next stage to detect cycles that are dominant and genuine within this filtered dataset.

However, we must treat the output from this pure mechanical detrending algorithm with care, as it is well known that this technique can generate spurious cycle variations; that is, the HP filter may generate cyclical dynamics even if none are present in the original data. Thus, the presence of cycles in HP-filtered data does not imply that real cycles exist in the original data. Therefore, we must apply additional mechanisms to validate identified genuine cycles and eliminate potential "invalid" cycles. Later, in Step 3 of our Scanner Cycle framework, we will show how to bypass this issue by including goodness-of-fit statistics for our genuine dominant cycle filtering.

To optimize the HP filter and minimize these spurious cycle deficiencies, first, the proper adjustment of the parameter "$\lambda$" in the HP filter decomposition is important. Second, additional tests are needed on how the estimated cyclical components behave based on cross-correlation evaluations to differentiate "genuine" from "false" cycles. Both adjustments have been incorporated into our Scanner Cycle framework to compensate for the disadvantages of the HP filter. We provide a detailed example in Section 9 of Chapter 2 with a virtual dataset of different cycles, trends, and random noise to demonstrate that the implemented variant can detect the most dominant genuine cycles included in the dataset.

A review of critical discussions about the HP filter method, however, indicates that the HP filter is likely to remain the standard for trend removal for a long time to come. Ravn and Uhlig concluded in 1997 as follows:

> "None of the defects and undesirable properties are particularly compelling: the HP filter has withstood the test of time and the fire of discussion extraordinarily well."

To further optimize the detrending preprocessing, additional findings based on Jim Hamilton's (2016) work could be considered. However, the HP filter enjoys broad support in the scientific community and is widely used. We have been able to successfully use the approach for years in cycle forecasting: Never change a running system. Therefore, it is strongly recommended that anyone looking to reconstruct a similar or more optimized detrending framework conduct further research in this area.

### 3.2 Step 2: Cycle Detection

Now that we have the cyclical dataset prepared, the next step is to discover the individual cycles that are active. Subsequently, the engine needs to perform a spectral analysis and then isolate the cycles that are repetitive and have the largest amplitudes. For this, we must decide on a cycle detection algorithm suited to our goal. Most cycle researchers are familiar with the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), and many "FFT-based engines" are available to detect one or more cycles in datasets. What many may not know, however, is that there is a special subset: the Goertzel algorithm.

Originally, the algorithm was used to detect "dominant" tone frequencies employed in landline telephones for DTMF signaling, developed long before the era of smartphones. Have you ever wondered how the telephone exchange knows which button was pressed? The answer is the Goertzel algorithm. Today, the Goertzel algorithm is widely used in communications for tone detection and is integrated into hardware as circuits to detect button tones in near real-time. Moreover, and more importantly, the Goertzel algorithm was originally designed to detect cycles in datasets with characteristics similar to current financial time series data. The problem at the time was detecting a specific tone within a very short span of available data, with considerable noise.

So, instead of using standard Fourier or wavelet transforms, why not use a well-established variant of the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT): the Goertzel algorithm? Since our requirements for cycle detection in financial markets are similar to those Goertzel addressed in the case of old telephone lines?

Our research shows that the Goertzel algorithm provides reliable results in decoding dominant cycles from detrended financial datasets, outperforming other methods such as wavelets or MESA.

Of course, the Goertzel DFT (GDFT) must be applied in a special way, as it is necessary to test GDFT across all possible wavelengths and use different methods to obtain the current phase and amplitude. That is, to cover a full cycle length spectrum, the Goertzel algorithm has greater complexity than FFT algorithms. However, using the Goertzel algorithm to obtain the dominant cycle length from short and noisy data, along with standard versions to obtain the related current phase and amplitude for the detected cycle length, helps generate all dynamic cycle data for the active cycle at the last point of our dataset in question.

Since we are not interested in the "average" cycle duration from longer datasets, we want the cycle length and phase that are active in the last bar of the chart. Therefore,

This approach is supported by a study conducted by Dennis Meyers (2003) on the Goertzel method:

> "With very noisy data where noise intensity is greater than signal intensity, [...] only the Goertzel algorithm can successfully identify the frequencies present."

Furthermore, we can observe an increasing amount of noise entering financial markets. Examples include high-frequency trading, purely algorithm-based trading engines, or alternative news sources. Thus, in real-world environments, we will not see "clean" financial datasets as they are diluted by noise that obscures the underlying real cycles. The Meyers study shows that the GDFT even outperforms the "MESA" method proposed by John F. Ehlers, used in most of his cycle research.

Therefore, our Scanner Cycle framework applies the GDFT to the detrended data set to cover all possible cycle lengths. Once the most active cycle is detected based on the full GDFT spectrum analysis, we use an additional step to check the current phase state at the last bar of our dataset with a shorter subset of the original full dataset, since we are interested in the state of the detected cycle length at the point of analysis, or the last available bar.

### 3.3 Step 3: Cycle Validation

After completing Step 2, we obtain a list of detected active cycles. Step 2 provided us with a list of cycles including length, amplitude, and current phase state at the end of the dataset. Now, we need to validate and rank these cycles as our focus is on identifying the most dominant cycles from this list.

Before starting any classification, let us revisit what was introduced in Step 1:

Based on the pitfalls of the HP filter, we need to verify the detected cycles using a second algorithm to validate whether a cycle is genuine or potentially spurious. Therefore, this step is important to avoid "virtual" cycles that are not present in the original dataset and are merely artifacts of the detrending algorithm itself.

Thus, we apply a special form of statistical correlation analysis for each detected cycle length. In this step, the statistical reliability of each cycle is evaluated.

The goal of the algorithm is to exclude cycles that have been influenced by one-time events (such as random news) and cycles that are not genuine.

One of the algorithms used for this purpose is a more sophisticated Bartels test. The test is based on mathematics (detailed statistics) and measures the stability of amplitude and phase for each cycle.

Bartels’ test for periodicity, published in the Carnegie Institution of Washington in 1932, was embraced by the Foundation for the Study of Cycles decades ago as the single best test for a given cycle's projected reliability, robustness, and consequently, utility.

It was originally published in 1935 by Julius Bartels in Volume 40, No. 1 of the scientific journal "Terrestrial Magnetism and Atmospheric Electricity" under the title "Random Fluctuations, Persistence, and Quasi-Persistence in Geophysics and Cosmic Physics." Later, Charles E. Armstrong provided a brief example and case study on applying the Bartels test to financial time series data in 1973, titled "Application of the Bartels Test of Significance to a Cycle Analysis of Time Series."

The Bartels test returns a value that measures the probability of a cycle's authenticity: values range from 0 to 1, and the lower the value, the less likely this cycle is due to chance or randomness.

The test considers both the consistency and persistence of a given cycle within the dataset it is applied to.

To make it more human-readable, we look for an easy-to-read indication of whether the cycle is genuine. We simply convert the raw Bartels value into a percentage indicating the probability that the cycle is genuine using the formula:
$$Genuine Cycle \% = (1 - Bartels Score) * 100$$

This gives us a value between 0% (random) and 100% (true).

This test now helps us filter out possible cycles that may have been detected in the cycle detection stage (Step 2) but were only present in the data series for a short period or by chance and should, therefore, not be considered dominant cycles in the original underlying data series.

Since we have a final percentage score, we just need to define an individual threshold below which cycles should be omitted. We recommend using a threshold of > 49%, so cycles with a Bartels genuine percentage value below 49% should be skipped by any subsequent forecasting or cycle analysis technique.

### 3.4 Step 4: Classification

A final important step in making sense of cyclical information is establishing a measure for the strength of a cycle. Once the previous step is complete, we have cycles that are dominant (based on their amplitude) and genuine (considering their driving force in the financial market). For trading purposes, this is not sufficient. The price influence of a cycle per bar on the trading chart is the most important information.

Let me give you some examples comparing two cycles. One cycle has a wavelength of 110 bars and an amplitude of 300. The other cycle has a wavelength of 60 bars and an amplitude of only 200.

Thus, if we apply the "standard" method to determine the dominant cycle—that is, selecting the cycle with the highest amplitude—we would choose the cycle with a wavelength of 110 and an amplitude of 300. But let us look at the following information—the cycle strength per bar:

- Wavelength 110 / Amplitude 300 = Strength per bar: 300/110 = 2.7
- Wavelength 60 / Amplitude 200 = Strength per bar: 200/60 = 3.3

For trading, it is more important to know which cycle has the greatest influence on driving the price per bar, not just which cycle has the highest amplitude!

That is why I introduce the "Cycle Strength" measurement value. Our cycle scanner calculates this value.

That said, to build a ranking based on the remaining cycles, it is recommended to rank these cycles based on their "influence" per price bar. Since we are looking for the most dominant cycles, these are the cycles that influence price movement the most per single bar.

Sort the results according to the calculated cycle strength score. Now we have a top-down list of cycles that have the highest influence on price movements per bar. And that is precisely what we need!

### 3.5 Summary: The Dominant Market Vibration

After the cycle analysis engine has completed all four stages, the top of the list (with the highest cycle strength score) provides us with information about the dominant vibration in the market.

Indeed, the wavelength of this cycle is the dominant vibration in the market, which is highly useful for trading. However, the result is not limited to the cycle length (based on our settings in Step 2, we not only have the dominant cycle length) but we also know—and this is the critically important current phase state of this cycle—(Important: not the averaged phase over the full dataset) but the instantaneous phase active at the time of analysis or the last available bar).

This allows us to provide more valid cycle projections on the "right" side of the chart for trading rather than using the "average" phase state typically applied across the full dataset for this cycle.
