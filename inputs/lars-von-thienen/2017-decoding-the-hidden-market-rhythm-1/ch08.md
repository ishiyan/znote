# Decoding the Hidden Market Rhythm

## 8. Opinion Cycles: Measuring and Trading Fear and Greed

In 1949, investing legend Benjamin Graham eloquently characterized the cyclical nature of financial markets in his book “The Intelligent Investor”:

> “The market is a pendulum that forever swings between unsustainable optimism (which makes stocks too expensive) and unjustified pessimism (which makes them too cheap). The intelligent investor is a realist who sells to optimists and buys from pessimists.”

Today, the emerging field of sentiment datasets supports Graham’s view by providing a solid empirical basis for the overreaction bias that often drives cyclical markets.

Typically, mood rises and falls positively and negatively. Therefore, sentiment increases and decreases in the form of dynamic cycles. Social mood refers to a feeling, emotion, or attitude about something and, of course, can span a range of values. Whenever mood is related to companies, the economy, or assets, the character of events will play out in the related financial assets. Fear and despondency represent one extreme, while excitement and euphoria represent the other end of the spectrum. Understanding the pendulum of greed and fear is the best tool a trader can have at their disposal.

Cycles are the important structure here, because sentiment does not jump quickly from one state to another—like a pendulum.

![Figura 8-1](./assets/fig-8-1.png)

**Figure 8-1:** Market pendulum that always swings between bullish and bearish extremes. [10]

A change in mood takes time; therefore, confidence moves in dynamic cycles or waves. This is a process similar to changes in air temperature: the outside temperature does not jump from one state to another. Thus, the challenge is to detect and forecast the extreme turning points called “Maximum Financial Risk” (Thrill/Euphoria) and “Maximum Financial Opportunity” (Panic/Capitulation).

A fascinating aspect of market cyclicality is that the average investor badly underperforms across the board because he or she is prone to chase performance near market tops and panic near market bottoms. For this reason, an effective measure of sentiment can be of great value in identifying the hot spots of maximum financial risk or opportunity within the dominant sentiment cycle.

Or, to use one of Warren’s most famous quotes:

> “Be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful.”

Accordingly, if you have datasets that provide raw “fear and greed” mood information related to financial assets on the one hand, and on the other hand have cyclical tools that can decipher and track dominant cycles, you have the tools needed to predict and forecast sentiment “hot spots.” Getting a handle on “financial stress” cycles in the market would substantially improve trading skill.

This chapter will provide trading examples based on sentiment cycles using:

- the Volatility Index (“VIX”),
- social media sentiment (“Twitter”), and
- the Financial Stress Index (Fed of St. Louis, symbol: STLFSI).

### 8.1 Trading “Fear” Volatility Cycles

A popular measure of market confidence for the S&P 500 is the Chicago Board Options Exchange Volatility Index (symbol: VIX). It is often referred to as the fear index.

The VIX measures volatility and has an inverse relationship with the stock market. The more the VIX value increases, the more panic there is in the market. The more the VIX decreases in value, the more complacency there is in the market.

As a measure of complacency and panic, the VIX is often used as a contrarian indicator. Extremely low VIX readings indicate a high degree of complacency and are generally considered bearish. Conversely, extremely high VIX readings indicate a high degree of anxiety or even panic and are considered bullish.

It would be useful to detect trader confidence cycles. Because emotions swing from panic selling to overconfident buying in the form of cycles, confidence indicators are a useful vehicle to transfer our cycle toolkit to. If we are able to forecast the expected sentiment highs or lows with our cycle analysis approach, we can use this leading information for trading purposes. We will use conditions on February 20, 2009 for our example.

### 8.2 Cycles within Cycles: Combining Price and Volatility Cycles

The following example combines price and volatility cycles. In this case, the VIX cycle is used afterward to verify and confirm possible cycle changes in the main price series. This demonstrates the importance of cross-checking detected cycles across related datasets. More importantly, it shows that dominant sentiment cycles can provide a precursor to price cycle changes.

#### The dominant carrier wave on the price chart – October 2010

I will use a “dynamic intermarket cycles-within-cycles” example to demonstrate the power of this approach.

We start in October 2010 with the long-term perspective on the weekly S&P 500 index chart.

The Dynamic Cycle Explorer is attached to the chart. The indicator detects the dominant cycle and checks for possible shifts in length and phase for this carrier wave on each bar. The example is based on the pure standard settings of this fully automated indicator—there is no way to over-optimize or curve-fit this tool. You would have arrived at exactly the same situation if you had attached it to the chart yourself on the given day. The dominant carrier wave is represented as a theoretical blue cycle at the bottom of the chart and automatically extends into the future. If we look at the past, we can see that the detected cycle fits very well with the peaks and troughs in price history.

![Figura 8-11](./assets/fig-8-11.png)

**Figure 8-11:** Weekly S&P 500 with the dominant cycle and ETA projection (October 8, 2010)

What interests us is the next ETA point, represented by the blue dotted line, the word ETA, and the purple band: The next major top is expected to arrive around April 29, 2011. Accordingly, we are in an expansion phase expected to last for the next 6 months from now. That is the picture the current cycle analysis gives us—the long-term carrier wave that will guide us on when to trade on the daily chart. Now we will observe the ETA point week by week to check whether the projection will change or not. We do not use this analysis as a one-off, fixed projection that cannot be modified. Instead, we update this projection every week once we get the next bar. This means we will follow the main dominant cycle and check whether its length or phase has changed, which could lead to an adjusted ETA projection.

I compare this type of analysis to Garmin navigation in your car. It provides the current estimated time of arrival at your destination. This arrival time is continuously updated in real time based on given traffic conditions. This is precisely what we will see with the dominant cycle: We will review its current state on each bar and adjust our ETA projection if necessary. Therefore, as we move forward in time, our ETA point will become more and more precise, bar by bar.

This is a valid high signal showing that the dominant cycle remained stable and that we can determine the peak of the carrier wave we have been following since October 2010. To answer this question, we apply the cycles-within-cycles approach. To time the trade, we must move to the next lower time frame. The Volatility Index (VIX) is a useful measure of market sentiment. ### 8.5 Trading with financial stress cycles

Understanding financial stress sentiment cycles is fundamental to generating returns in the current market environment. Sentiment cycles influence the movement of financial markets and are directly related to people’s moods. Getting a handle on “financial stress” cycles in the market would substantially improve trading skill. Fortunately, the St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index (symbol: STLFSI) is a dataset that provides information about the current level of “financial stress” in the market. This chapter highlights the importance of detecting cycles in “financial stress” datasets to detect turning points in financial indexes. The following case study exemplifies the importance of sentiment cycles and the predictive capability of the Dynamic Cycle Explorer once again. The Dynamic Cycle Explorer operates on the assumption that cycles are not static over time. Let’s recap the DCE approach introduced in the chapter: Introduction to the Dynamic Cycle Explorer method:

> Whenever a new bar appears on the chart, the Dynamic Cycle Explorer re-evaluates the state of the current dominant cycle in terms of length, strength, and phasing. Subsequently, it updates this cycle by plotting it into future projections. However, a trader will focus only on the next expected turning point, which is what a market analyst cares about. The DCE is not used to predict a complete static cycle into the future. We are interested in determining and monitoring the next turning point based on the phasing of the detected dominant carrier wave—the point in time where the market is expected to turn.
>
> As we move forward in time, each bar means an update of the expected turning point via a new assessment of the current situation of the dominant cycle’s length and phase. This dynamic forecast based on the current state of the dominant cycle provides information about the timing and direction of the next turning point. We get real-time information about when to expect the next turning point in the market as we continuously re-evaluate the dominant carrier wave’s parameters. This information is updated every time a new bar appears.

This technique was used in a market report published on June 8, 2014 in the publicly available WhenToTrade magazine section. [11]

The St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index (STLFSI) is a vehicle that can be used to analyze confidence data. It was created using principal component analysis, a statistical method for extracting the factors responsible for the correlation of a set of variables. Financial stress has been identified as the main factor influencing the joint movement of the designated market variables; extracting this factor allows the St. Louis Fed to create an interpretable index. The index is constructed using weekly data series for a variety of interest rates, credit spreads, and volatility measures.

### 8.7 “All-in!” – mapping sentiment cycles to price developments: The case

As shown in the previous chapter, an ideal scenario for teaching investors to look for cycle alignments involves considering multiple related sentiment datasets. It is never advisable to trade solely on one cycle from our DCE toolkit. Moreover, trading when certain sentiment cycles align perfectly illustrates (a) how market topping processes take time and (b) that a cycle projection will never be absolutely precise. These cycles do, however, provide us with clues suggesting that a market top may be approaching. In this way, these cycles can be treated as leading indicators for predicting market tops. The Elliott Wave International team echoes these sentiments:

> “We often refer to stock market tops as a process because they occur over time. The reason is that the emotion underlying a peak is hope, which dissipates diffusely. Some stock indexes and sectors top before others, peeling away from the market uptrend.”
>
> Elliott Wave International

This chapter summarizes another real juncture published in the public WTT online magazine on March 29, 2015. The post was intended to be an advance alert of an imminent market top. A few days after the post, the social media sentiment cycle (provided by PsychSignal) aligned with the VIX (fear) cycle forecast. The alignment between PsychSignal, VIX, and price cycles gave a clear message to investors: “All in!”

You should already be familiar with the DCE approach. Next, we add a cycle-focused tool that can help in market analysis. This tool is intended to provide a way to facilitate validation of whether a cycle within a dataset correlates with actual sentiment turns in the related price series.

Specifically, we will demonstrate a cycle mapping feature included in the WTT charting platform. This feature allows direct mapping of cycles or indicator turns from one vehicle to another (without the need for coding or scripting), thereby providing the user with potential trading statistics associated with those cycles. By using this tool, the user can request performance statistics for the various cycles, visualized with different charts. This is useful if you want to perform cycle analysis on sentiment datasets and map that cycle’s turns to the related price chart under review. For example, if you are analyzing cycles in VIX data, turns of that cycle should be validated on the S&P 500 price chart. Typically, this can be done by converting the cycle’s lows and highs from the sentiment chart to the price chart.
