## 13. INTRODUCTION Genetic Algorithms for Detecting Market Cycles

### 13.1 Detecting Cycles in Financial Data with a Genetic Algorithm

Financial markets are cyclic, making it crucial for traders to identify current, dominant, and active cycles. Traditional routines from digital signal processing and frequency analysis are often applied to financial data series. However, these methods assume cycles remain static over long periods. In reality, cycles in financial time series are dynamic—shifting in length, amplitude, and phase over time. This dynamism complicates the accurate detection of dominant cycles using conventional signal processing. Cycle forecasts have traditionally relied on snapshots of the current active cycle, treating it as static and extrapolating it forward. This simplification oversimplifies market behavior, often leading to poorly estimated future cycles that ignore dynamic components.

A successful cycle-based trading approach must adapt to the dominant cycle’s evolving nature, continually adjusting forecasts—much like a GPS recalculates arrival times based on real-time speed, road, and traffic conditions.

Genetic algorithms (GAs) offer this adaptability by tracking market conditions and dynamically adjusting parameters based on underlying data. Unlike traditional signal processing, which evaluates all possible combinations, GAs use a natural evolutionary process to identify optimal solutions. They operate on principles of evolution, including "survival of the fittest," and are widely applicable to optimization problems. GAs mimic biological evolution as a problem-solving strategy, encoding potential solutions and evaluating them using a "fitness function." This function quantitatively assesses each candidate solution, allowing GAs to optimize functions with dynamic, nonlinear constraints (e.g., length, phase, amplitude). They are particularly effective for detecting dominant cycles.

To apply GAs to cycle detection, financial cycle mathematics must be encoded into the algorithm. Here, the cycle function acts as the chromosome, and its parameters—length, phase shift, and amplitude—serve as genes. For example, a genome might consist of chromosomes representing a sinusoidal wave, where each chromosome’s genes define specific cycle attributes. Over time, these genes evolve through crossover, mutation, and natural selection. A random population is created, comprising genomes with varying chromosomes and genes. A fitness function evaluates each genome’s quality, often based on trading performance (e.g., selling at cycle peaks, buying at cycle troughs).

From this population, the GA assesses candidates according to the fitness function. Poor performers are discarded, while fitter candidates—those yielding profitable trades—are retained. Promising cycles reproduce, with offspring occasionally undergoing random mutations. This new generation undergoes another round of fitness evaluation, with weaker candidates eliminated. Chance mutations sometimes improve fitness, and enhanced candidates are selected for reproduction. Iterating this process over hundreds or thousands of rounds uncovers robust cycle solutions optimized for trading performance. This approach allows GAs to evolve cycle parameters dynamically, aligning with natural evolutionary rules.

The result is a static cycle length for the fittest candidate, similar to outputs from digital signal processing. However, GAs offer additional benefits. Since a GA maintains a population of evolving candidates, the algorithm doesn’t need reapplication to the original dataset as new data arrives. The population continues evolving with incoming price data, allowing the fittest candidates to adapt continuously—mirroring the market’s natural dynamism. GAs have proven immensely powerful for solving complex problems efficiently across various fields.

### 13.2 Intraday Volume Cycles for E-mini S&P 500 Futures

Another way to analyze financial cycles is through traded volume. Intriguing correlations exist between fixed daily trading volumes and intraday price reversals. Static volume thresholds can be interpreted as follows: If all available traders go long (expressed as fixed traded volume), prices must decline temporarily to facilitate subsequent long liquidation. Detecting fixed volume figures explaining intraday price movements provides a trading edge.

Instead of time-based cycles, fixed-volume cycles offer a logical way to detect market turns.

However, once volume-price correlations are identified, they rarely remain static. Keeping pace with dominant volume cycles is challenging due to their dynamic nature. Daily volume cycles adjust based on available traders, representing the volume needing cover for price direction changes. Genetic algorithms (GAs) offer a promising alternative to digital signal processing for detecting volume cycles and incorporating daily trader flow. Similar to chromosomes in a genome, a GA evaluates cycle lengths for entry/exit signals. The genome transforms through evolution—mutation, crossover, and survival of the fittest. Starting with a random population of cycle genomes, the GA evolves to detect volume cycles at various starting points, optimizing them using natural evolution rules. Each genome’s fitness is measured by a function assessing equity curve performance: smooth, upward-sloping curves score highest.

This strategy requires a charting and analysis platform capable of plotting data, applying cycle analysis, using GAs, and triggering real-time alerts. The WhenToTrade (WTT) software meets these requirements. The next example demonstrates this strategy’s real-world application, recorded live before market hours.

An 8,000-volume chart for E-mini S&P 500 futures was used—each bar represents 8,000 traded contracts. The x-axis reflects fixed volume, not time, so cycle lengths correspond to fixed volume, not time spans.

On January 17, 2014, at 7:00 AM New York time—an hour before the U.S. market open—the GA was applied to the prior two weeks’ data to evolve cycle genomes. Figure 13-5 shows the setup. January 2–15 data was used for GA evolution, with January 15–16 out-of-sample data for validation.

![Figure 13-5](./assets/fig-13-5.png)

The Relative Strength Index (RSI) analyzed volume cycles on the price chart. RSI acts as an oscillator with cyclic behavior, making it suitable for cycle detection. Trading signals were generated when the RSI for volume bars crossed specific thresholds. Each signal had two adjustable parameters: RSI length and threshold value. The length-threshold combination measured active cycles via the RSI indicator. Different parameters were allowed for long, short, and exit signals, assuming distinct cycles for each. This flexibility synchronizes with real trader sentiment.

The generic script for applying this signal: `Cross(RSI(close, length), THRESHOLD)`

Next, the GA identified parameters generating consistently upward-sloping equity curves during the specified period. The GA ran for 3–5 minutes, identifying the fittest genomes (Figure 13-6).

![Figure 13-6](./assets/fig-13-6.png)

**Figure 13-6:** GA results after three minutes

GAs offer a key advantage over brute-force optimization: they efficiently search vast spaces. Brute-force methods check every parameter combination for local optima. Here, RSI lengths ranged from 5–35, and thresholds had 30 values, yielding 900 combinations per signal. With four signals (long/short entry/exit), the search space included over 600 billion possible strategies. Brute-force evaluation of all combinations is impractical for intraday trading, where results are needed pre-market. In contrast, the GA detected profitable combinations within three minutes, guided by natural evolution rules.

The GA identified RSI cycles in the volume chart yielding a 76% return (86 E-mini points) from January 2–15. The equity curve maintained a consistent upward slope, indicating steady profits and low risk. Even the out-of-sample curve (right of the red line) showed promise.
